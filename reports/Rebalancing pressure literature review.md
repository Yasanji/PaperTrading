# Local rebalancing signals remain untested abroad

No academic paper found in this review builds local 60/40 rebalancing signals for Canada, Australia, the UK or Germany and tests whether they predict next-day local equity-minus-bond returns, whether pre-registered or not. The closest prior work is Harvey, Mazzoleni and Melone (HMM) itself. Its international section (Section 3.5, Table D.14) regresses foreign equity index returns on **US-built** Threshold and Calendar signals. It finds negative, significant coefficients with R² of about 2.5%, but it builds no local signals and does not test foreign equity-minus-bond returns ([EDHEC PDF](https://www.edhec.edu/sites/default/files/2026-03/Scientific%20paper.%20ssrn-5122748.pdf); [EDHEC slides](https://www.edhec.edu/sites/default/files/2026-03/slides_EDHEC_Michele%20Mazzoleni%20%281%29.pdf)). The three concurrent papers HMM cite (Lu and Wu; Sammon and Shim; Chen) are all US-focused, and none builds country-level signals. The only replication found is a US-only practitioner Substack post. The gap is real but narrow. A local-signal paper must still separate rebalancing pressure from an unconditional turn-of-month premium, month-end liquidity demand, bond index-extension flows and spillover from the US signal. It must also use persistence-robust, few-country and multiple-testing-adjusted inference. Two limits apply to the novelty claim. The search was budget-limited (about 15 tool calls per strand), and Google Scholar or Semantic Scholar "cited by" lists for HMM were never retrieved, so "not found" means "not found in this pass", not proof that nothing exists.

Verification markings from the notes are kept throughout. **[V]** or **[CR]** means checked in the research session; [CR] specifically means checked against Crossref on 2026-10-11. **[U]** or **[UNVERIFIED]** means from background knowledge, with venue, pages and DOI still to be checked. Where the notes disagree, the disagreement is stated.

## HMM's own international test uses US signals, and nobody has gone local

**Harvey, C.R., Mazzoleni, M.G. and Melone, A. (2025, rev. Jan 2026), "The Unintended Consequences of Rebalancing", NBER WP 33554, DOI 10.3386/w33554; SSRN 5122748 [V].** Institutions rebalance on calendar schedules or when allocations drift past thresholds. When equities are overweight, **next-day equity returns are about 17 bp lower**. Momentum, reversal and macro controls do not remove the effect, and the authors put the cost at about **$16bn a year** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33554)). The paper has several versions: first posted January 2025, an AFA copy dated December 15, 2025, and an EDHEC-hosted copy dated January 14, 2026. It was presented at AFA 2026, in a session whose listed discussants include Jonathan Parker; the program does not say which discussant was assigned to HMM ([AFA](https://afajof.org/management/viewp.php?n=144452); [ASSA 2026 program](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2026/program/1943)). The notes say a Journal of Finance revise-and-resubmit status could not be confirmed from any source. One author (Mazzoleni) is at Capital Group, and the notes record a disclosure.

The international test is the most important piece of prior work for the proposed paper. HMM regress "international equity indices" on the US signals, lagged one day because foreign markets close earlier, and control for two-day trailing S&P 500 returns. EDHEC slide 32 reports a **Threshold coefficient of about −0.27 to −0.28 (s.e. about 0.09)** and a **Calendar×week4 coefficient of about −0.21 (s.e. about 0.05)**, with about 6,096–6,098 observations and adjusted R² of about 0.025–0.027 ([EDHEC slides](https://www.edhec.edu/sites/default/files/2026-03/slides_EDHEC_Michele%20Mazzoleni%20%281%29.pdf)). The observation count implies a single aggregate index with about 24 years of daily data, perhaps EAFE or ACWI ex-US. This is an **inference, not verified**, and the country list and sample period were not visible in the material accessed. Economically, HMM's test captures **US investors' flows into foreign equities**, not local pension rebalancing. That is the central contrast for the proposed paper: local signals predicting local equity-minus-bond returns, with the US signal as a control.

HMM's robustness appendix covers delta ranges (D.6), 50/50, 70/30 and 86/14 weights (D.7), nonlinearity and autocorrelation (D.8), ARA controls (D.9), reversals (D.10) and a Bloomberg Aggregate benchmark (D.4). It puts rebalancing costs at about 8 bp a year (Calendar about 5 bp, Threshold about 15 bp, or 11 bp on a conservative assumption) ([EDHEC PDF](https://www.edhec.edu/sites/default/files/2026-03/Scientific%20paper.%20ssrn-5122748.pdf)). A pre-registration should borrow these variants so that its specification choices are anchored to the original paper.

## Flow, calendar and inelastic-markets literatures predict the effect and also compete with it

**Pension and TDF rebalancing.** Three papers give the strongest causal backing for the mechanism. **Parker, J.A., Schoar, A. and Sun, Y. (2023), "Retail Financial Innovation and Stock Market Dynamics: The Case of Target Date Funds", *JF* 78(5), 2673–2723, DOI 10.1111/jofi.13258** [V for title, NBER WP and JF; volume, pages and DOI U] shows that TDFs' contrarian rebalancing causes temporary price pressure and reversals in stocks they hold heavily ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28028)). **Da, Z., Larrain, B., Sialm, C. and Tessada, J. (2018), "Destabilizing Financial Advice", *RFS* 31(10), 3720–3755** [V title and repository; venue details U] is the closest non-US case. Coordinated switching between Chile's equity-heavy fund A and bond-heavy fund E moved Chilean equities, bonds and FX, and the moves later reversed ([UC repository](https://repositorio.uc.cl/dspace/items/c5341f83-8b9f-4c79-a4a0-991b31bce67a)). **Tyagi, Schotman and Bams (2016), "Asset allocation dynamics of pension funds", Netspar DP 016** [V] uses international CEM-type data covering the US, Canada and Europe. Most equity-weight changes are passive drift, and rebalancing is **asymmetric: weak after equity gains, strong after losses**. US and DB funds rebalance less ([Netspar PDF](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/P20160320_dp016_Tyagi.pdf)). The two note files list this one paper under different author orders. One strand reports that funds rebalance about 80–90% of drift each year. Parker and Sun (2025) and Chinco and Fos (2021, RAPS) are also cited by HMM and are US only.

**Month-end and turn-of-month (TOM) effects.** These are the most dangerous competing explanation. **Kayaçetin, N.V. (2026), "Infrequent rebalancing, risk deferral, and equity returns at the turn of the month", *JIFMIM* 109, 102309** [V] studies 30 countries over 1994–2023. Mean daily returns around month-end are **about 10 bp versus about 0 otherwise**, and a narrow window around month-end accounts for nearly all of the positive return in each country. Kayaçetin attributes this to synchronized rebalancing mandates and risk deferral, with stronger effects after turbulence ([Işık repository](https://acikerisim.isikun.edu.tr/entities/publication/5edc97f4-48ac-48e2-97e3-6057f70b2ecb/full)). That paper already makes an international rebalancing claim, but an unconditional one: it does not condition on the sign of 60/40 drift. **Etula, E., Rinne, K., Suominen, M. and Vaittinen, L. (2020), "Dash for Cash", *RFS* 33(1), 75–111** [V] attributes month-end return patterns in the US and abroad, including Treasuries, to institutional cash needs ([OUP](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/33/1/75/5494694)); its exact window lengths are [U]. The classic TOM papers are Ariel (1987, *JFE*) [U], Lakonishok and Smidt (1988, *RFS*) [U] and McConnell and Xu (2008, *FAJ*; TOM in 30 of 35 countries) [U]. Practitioner sources document **month-end duration extensions** in USTs, gilts and EGBs when bond indices rebalance ([ITC Markets](https://www.itcmarkets.com/itc-egb-gilt-and-ust-month-end-extensions/)). This mechanically lifts bond returns at month-end and therefore affects the equity-minus-bond spread. No peer-reviewed measurement was verified; Hartley and Schwarz, "Predictable End-of-Month Treasury Returns", is an unread lead.

**Window dressing.** Lakonishok, Shleifer, Thaler and Vishny (1991, AER P&P) [U], Ng and Wang (2004, JFE) [U] and Carhart, Kaniel, Musto and Reed (2002, JF, "Leaning for the Tape") [U] document quarter-end price marking with reversals. This is a further quarter-end confound for the Calendar signal.

**Cross-asset FX and international equity rebalancing.** **Camanho, N., Hau, H. and Rey, H. (2022), "Global Portfolio Rebalancing and Exchange Rates", *RFS* 35(11), 5228–5274** [V title and venue; volume and pages U] covers about 7,940 equity funds in the US, UK, Eurozone and Canada. Funds rebalance out of foreign markets after foreign excess returns, and the flows move currencies ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3127061); [NBER draft](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f127643/f127643.pdf)). This is the nearest multi-country rebalancing work, but its outcome is FX, not local equity-minus-bond returns. Hau and Rey (2006, RFS) [U] and (2004, AER P&P) [U] supply the theory. For Canadian, Australian and UK 60/40 funds with large foreign sleeves, the local signal also implies FX flows, which gives a natural secondary test.

**Inelastic markets.** **Gabaix, X. and Koijen, R.S.J., "In Search of the Origins of Financial Fluctuations: The Inelastic Markets Hypothesis", NBER WP 28967** [V title and WP] argues that fixed-share mandates make aggregate demand inelastic. The widely quoted **multiplier of about 5** is [U], and journal publication was not confirmed ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28967)). **Koijen and Yogo (2019), "A Demand System Approach to Asset Pricing", *JPE* 127(4), 1475–1515** [CR in the methods notes; JPE details U in the flows notes] estimates inelastic institutional demand ([Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1086/701683)). These papers let the authors set an expected magnitude ex ante, which strengthens a pre-registration.

**Sovereign funds and country evidence.** The notes found **no verified academic price-impact study of NBIM or GPIF rebalancing**. Hasse, Lecourt and Siagh (2023), AMSE WP 2322, compares rebalancing rules for the Norwegian benchmark on performance, not price pressure ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/aim/wpaimx/2322.html)). Australia has the RBA's April 2021 FSR Box C on 2020 super switching and liquidity pressure, which documents the episode without estimating price impact ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2021/apr/box-c-what-did-2020-reveal-about-liquidity-challenges-facing-superannuation-funds.html)). There is also an APRA paper on member choice whose existence only is verified ([APRA](https://www.apra.gov.au/sites/default/files/Peng-Determinants-of-member-choice-in-the-Australian-superannuation-industry.pdf)). For the UK, Pinter (2023), BoE Staff WP 1019, on the 2022 LDI gilt crisis [U], covers collateral-driven forced selling, not calendar rebalancing. No Canadian or German study was found. An IJCB 6(2) paper on pension rebalancing and free float exists, but its content was not read ([IJCB](https://www.ijcb.org/sites/default/files/journal/v6n2/ijcb-v6n2-stock-market-performance-and-pension-fund-investment-policy-rebalancing-free-float-or.pdf)). Sell-side estimates of quarter-end pension selling, such as JPMorgan's figure of up to $316bn, are not academic tests ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/business/markets/jpmorgan-says-up-to-316b-selling-in-equites-by-quarter-end?amp=1)).

## Replication and decay literatures frame this as a confirmatory test, and PBFJ fits best

The paper can sit between two camps. The "crisis" camp includes **Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016), *RFS* 29(1), 5–68** [U in replication notes; CR in methods notes], with its t > 3 hurdle ([Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhv059)), and Hou, Xue and Zhang (2020, *RFS*, "Replicating Anomalies") [U]. The "replicates" camp includes **Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen (2023), "Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?", *JF* 78(5), 2465–2518** [V], which finds most factors replicate across 93 countries ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28432)), and Chen and Zimmermann (2022, *CFR*) [U]. **Menkveld et al. (2024), "Nonstandard Errors", *JF* 79(3), 2339–2390** [V; DOI U] shows sizable dispersion across 164 analyst teams working on the same data ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3961574)), which is the clearest argument for fixing the specification in advance. Harvey's 2017 AFA Presidential Address recommends pre-registration [U]. That is pointed framing here, since Harvey is an author of the paper being tested.

On decay, McLean and Pontiff (2016, *JF*) [U] report returns about 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication. **Jacobs and Müller** (WP 2017 [V]; JFE 2020 [U]) find that a reliable post-publication decline appears only in the US, not in 38 other markets ([WP PDF](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/fofi2018/files/2018/03/FoFI-2018-0174-Sebastian-M%25C3%25BCller2.pdf)). HMM were publicized in early 2025, so an out-of-sample window after publication adds a decay test. Under Jacobs and Müller, the US signal should decay more than local foreign signals, where arbitrage capital is thinner. Other canonical international out-of-sample papers are Hou, Karolyi and Kho (2011) [U], Fama and French (2012) [U] and Rouwenhorst (1998) [U]. **No pre-registered international anomaly replication in finance was located**, so a "first" claim needs more searching.

On venues, **Pacific-Basin Finance Journal** has the clearest pathway. Its pre-registration initiative evaluates the question and method before data are analysed, through a two-phase pitch at partner conferences ([Corvinus CFP](https://www.uni-corvinus.hu/contents/uploads/2024/05/2024LiquidityConference_PBFJ_Pre-registeredResearchInitiative_FINAL_9May2024%20(1).80f.pdf)) [V]. PBFJ already publishes pre-registered reports on replication ([Chai et al. 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pacfin/v86y2024ics0927538x24002051.html); [Hasso et al. 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pacfin/v91y2025ics0927538x24003160.html)) [V], and Australia is in the sample. Whether direct submissions outside conference partnerships are accepted in 2025–26 is unverified. **Critical Finance Review** runs editor-selected replication issues, so the authors should approach the editor ([CFR](https://cfr.ivo-welch.info/home/replication-issues.html)) [V]. **JCRE** is free and open access and accepts "generalizations", but its page does not mention finance or pre-registration ([ZBW](https://www.zbw.eu/en/research-data/services/journal-of-comments-and-replications-in-economics)) [V]. I4R runs a discussion-paper series and replication games ([World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/impactevaluations/new-opportunities-replications-guest-post-abel-brodeur)) [V]. Replication or registered-report policies at Economics Letters and Finance Research Letters could not be verified because their pages were blocked. JF and RFS code policies are [U], and neither is known to run a registered-report track.

## Referees will demand IVX, few-cluster panel inference and reversal paths

A drift-based signal is built mechanically from past returns, so its innovations correlate with return innovations. That is exactly where the **Stambaugh (1999, *JFE* 54(3), 375–421)** [CR] bias is worst ([Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-405X(99)00041-0)). The notes recommend reporting persistence ρ̂ and innovation correlation δ̂ per country, which is an inference from the methods literature. Three points carry the most weight. First, **a pooled estimate does not remove Stambaugh bias** (Hjalmarsson), and no verified panel-IVX reference was found. Second, with simulated signals, "out of sample" mainly means that the signal is feasible in real time. A referee will check that no future data enter the signal before looking at R² values, and Inoue and Kilian (2005) [CR] note that out-of-sample tests alone do not prevent data mining ([Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1081/ETC-200040785)). Third, Boudoukh, Richardson and Whitelaw (2008) [CR] warn that estimates at different horizons are not independent evidence ([Crossref](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhl042)). Giacomini and Rossi (2010) is [UNVERIFIED], and the Pesaran-Timmermann (1992) directional test was not verified.

## No formal critique of HMM exists, but four weaknesses are visible

The notes found **no published comment, rebuttal or discussant slides**. The AFA 2026 discussion should be obtained. Four lines of critique can be inferred from the notes. The first is signal realism: Tyagi, Schotman and Bams show that real rebalancing is partial and asymmetric, so a fully mechanical 60/40 signal overstates flows, especially after equity rallies. This also gives a testable prediction that the effect should be larger after drawdowns. The second is the counterfactual: the $16bn cost has no stated baseline. The third is calendar overlap: the Calendar×week4 signal shares timing with TOM, Dash-for-Cash and bond-extension seasonality, which practitioners trade on their own. The fourth is the scope of the international evidence: HMM's foreign test uses one aggregate index and US-built signals, so it cannot separate a global US-flow channel from local mandates.

## Conclusion

The contribution is novel in a specific way. It asks whether **local** mandates (Australian super, UK DB and LDI schemes, Canada's large DB plans, German institutions) produce **local** equity-minus-bond pressure, controlling for the US signal that HMM already showed spills abroad. Neither HMM's Table D.14, Kayaçetin's 30-country TOM study nor Camanho, Hau and Rey answers this question. The closest prior work is HMM Section 3.5, followed by Kayaçetin (2026) and Da et al. (2018). The design gives an identification lever none of them has. Local and US drift diverge whenever local markets decouple from the US, so a horse race between the two signals tests whether the price pressure is local or global.

The paper must rule out five competing explanations. The first is the **unconditional TOM premium** (Kayaçetin; Etula et al.): interact drift sign with TOM dummies and show that the effect flips sign with drift. The second is **bond index extension** at month-end: decompose the spread into its equity and bond legs. The third is **US-signal spillover**: include HMM's lagged US signals. The fourth is **quarter-end window dressing**. The fifth is **information rather than pressure**: show full reversal in cumulative local projections. Pre-registering these tests, with a single pooled IVX slope as the primary test and a post-January-2025 holdout, turns a crowded month-end literature into a confirmatory test. It also provides a direct Jacobs-Müller-style check of whether HMM's signal decays faster in the US than abroad after publication. Before claiming priority, the authors should run an SSRN full-text search and check Google Scholar's "cited by" list for SSRN 5122748.
